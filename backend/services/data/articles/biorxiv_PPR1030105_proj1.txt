Abstract
Background
Mycobacterium fortuitum (M. fortuitum), a rapidly growing nontuberculous mycobacterium (NTM), is an
emerging pathogen in trauma-associated skin and soft tissue infections (SSTIs). Pediatric NTM
infections are rare and often misdiagnosed due to atypical presentations and intrinsic β-lactam
resistance. We report the first pediatric case of M. fortuitum necrotizing cellulitis following a dog bite in
an 8-year-old child, highlighting diagnostic and management challenges.
Case presentation
: An 8-year-old child presented with necrotizing cellulitis following a dog bite, which was refractory to
empiric β-lactam therapy. Histopathology revealed nodular granulomatous inflammation with epithelioid
cells and purulent necrosis, and acid-fast bacilli were identified via Ziehl-Neelsen staining. Metagenomic
next-generation sequencing (mNGS) rapidly identified M. fortuitum within 48 hours, confirmed by
automated liquid culture (7-day growth) and targeted PCR. A multidisciplinary approach integrating broth
microdilution-guided combination therapy (amikacin 0.5 g three times weekly, linezolid 0.3 g twice daily,
and doxycycline 0.1 g twice daily) with weekly or biweekly surgical debridement achieved complete
resolution of cutaneous lesions.
Conclusions
This case highlights three key points: (1) NTM infections should be considered in animal bite wounds
unresponsive to β-lactams; (2) mNGS accelerates pathogen detection, enabling timely susceptibility-
directed therapy; (3) Combined antimicrobial and surgical strategies are essential for successful
treatment. Early multidisciplinary consultation is crucial to prevent chronic infection or systemic
dissemination in complex SSTIs.

Background
Animal bites are a significant global health issue, accounting for 5% of trauma-related emergency visits,
with dog bites representing 80–90% of cases [1, 2]. Pediatric populations are particularly affected, with
337,000 annual U.S. emergency visits and $2 billion in healthcare costs [3]. While only 5–25% of bite
injuries develop significant infections [4], complications such as rabies prophylaxis and antimicrobial-
resistant skin and soft tissue infections (SSTIs) place a substantial strain on healthcare systems. The
Page 2/12
microbiology of bite wounds involves a mix of canine oral commensals, human cutaneous microbiota,
and environmental organisms, often leading to polymicrobial infections [5, 6]. Delayed presentations may
involve opportunistic or fastidious pathogens, including NTM, which are challenging to diagnose and
treat. M. fortuitum, a rapidly growing NTM with intrinsic resistance to β-lactams and macrolides,
exemplifies this challenge. Its clinical presentation mimics common pyogenic infections, and cultures
yield positive results in only 40%-60% of confirmed cases [7]. Effective management requires prolonged,
susceptibility-guided combination therapy [8]. This study reports the first pediatric case of M. fortuitum-
induced necrotizing cellulitis following a dog bite, highlighting diagnostic pitfalls and therapeutic
strategies. It underscores the need for early multidisciplinary consultation in managing complex SSTIs.
We present this case in accordance with the CARE reporting checklist.
Case Presentation
An 8-year-old girl (height 140 cm, weight 34 kg) was admitted on December 12, 2023, with a non-healing
right thigh wound complicated by sinus tract formation and continuous exudate, persisting for 2 months
after a domestic dog bite. Initial treatment at an external hospital included tetanus immunoglobulin,
rabies vaccination, and wound irrigation with suturing, but the wound failed to heal. Imaging
(MRI/ultrasound) revealed a subcutaneous abscess (3×2 cm) with surrounding soft tissue edema and
ipsilateral inguinal lymphadenopathy (short-axis diameter 1.0 cm). Wound cultures showed no
pathogenic bacterial growth.
On admission, physical examination revealed two residual dog bite wounds with purulent discharge from
a central sinus tract, erythema (4×3 cm), and tenderness in the right inguinal region. Laboratory tests
showed white blood cell count (WBC) 7.8×10⁹/L and neutrophil percentage (NEUT%) 65%. On December
13, she underwent debridement, inguinal lymphadenectomy, and vacuum-sealed drainage (VSD). Post-
operatively, she developed intermittent fever and was treated with intravenous amoxicillin-clavulanate
potassium (1 g every 8 hours). The clinical course is shown in Fig. 1. Histopathology examination of the
surgical specimens revealed epithelioid cell granulomatous inflammation with purulent necrosis (Fig. 2).
Acid-fast staining was positive (3–9 bacilli per 100 fields), but the tuberculosis antibody test was
negative. One week later, during the second debridement, the intraoperative specimen tested negative
for Mycobacterium tuberculosis DNA.
\
On December 27, during the third debridement, metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS)
identified Mycobacterium fortuitum within 48 hours (sequence count 14,155; confidence level 99%)
(Fig. 3). Following consultation with a clinical pharmacist, a treatment regimen was initiated, including
oral clarithromycin (0.5 g once daily), linezolid (0.3 g twice daily), and intravenous amikacin (0.5 g three
times weekly). However, wound healing was not as expected. Two weeks later, tissue from re-
debridement was cultured for 7 days, and M. fortuitum was confirmed by automated liquid culture and
PCR, with antimicrobial susceptibility results obtained (Table 1). The clinical pharmacist recommended
replacing clarithromycin with doxycycline. On January 17, during the fifth debridement, necrotic tissue
Page 3/12
decreased but acid-fast staining remained positive. Cefmetazole (1 g twice daily) was added, and
doxycycline was discontinued after 21 days due to elevated liver enzymes (alanine aminotransferase
[ALT] 80.0 U/L and aspartate aminotransferase [AST] 55.9 U/L). Two weeks later, during the sixth
debridement, the wound showed abundant granulation tissue, and liver enzymes had normalized.
The patient was discharged on February 12 and continued oral linezolid for 3 months. Follow-up showed
good wound healing with no discomfort.

Discussion
This case provides the first molecular evidence confirming a direct causal link between dog bites and M.
fortuitum infection, offering critical clinical evidence for the zoonotic transmission mechanism of NTM.
As environmental commensals, NTM are primarily transmitted through soil or water exposure, with
previous consensus suggesting no animal-to-human transmission [9]. Although domestic animals can
harbor NTM and develop self-infections [10, 11], human infections resulting from animal bites are
exceedingly rare, with only five documented cases of canine-derived infections in the literature[12–16].
This case, supported by existing evidence, demonstrates that oral NTM colonization in canines
constitutes a novel transmission risk. The underlying mechanisms involve traumatic inoculation leading
to biofilm formation, dysbiosis of the salivary microbiome promoting proliferation of opportunistic
pathogens, and immunodeficiency-related opportunistic infections [5, 6]. Dogs may act as potential
zoonotic vectors, underscoring the necessity for clinical reevaluation of infection risks associated with
animal-related trauma.
The clinical manifestations of NTM skin infections are relatively insidious, posing significant diagnostic
challenges. With an incubation period of 2–4 weeks, the pathological presentation involves
granulomatous inflammation combined with suppurative changes, which overlaps histologically with
tuberculosis infection. Traditional diagnostic methods for NTM—such as acid-fast staining (with low
sensitivity), prolonged culture cycles (4–6 weeks), and phenotypic differentiation difficulties [8, 17]—
highlight the limitations of conventional diagnostic systems. In this case, mNGS was employed to
accurately identify the pathogen within 48 hours. As a hypothesis-free and unbiased approach, mNGS
leverages high-throughput and rapid sequencing capabilities to detect and quantify all microorganisms
in a single specimen. Quantitative analysis effectively distinguishes pathogens from colonizing flora, as
demonstrated here by the detection of 14,155 specific sequences of M. fortuitum, significantly
surpassing background microbial signals. This technology overcomes the "blind spots" of clinical
Page 4/12
presumption, shortens the identification time for rare pathogens, and provides critical evidence for early
targeted therapeutic decisions [18, 19]. We recommend incorporating mNGS as an alternative diagnostic
tool for non-healing wounds (> 7 days) following animal bites or cases refractory to conventional
treatments.
The treatment of NTM skin infections requires individualized regimens integrating antimicrobial
susceptibility testing, tissue penetration profiles, and patient-specific factors [10]. M. fortuitum generally
exhibits resistance to first-line antituberculosis drugs, with amikacin demonstrating the strongest in vitro
antibacterial activity, while susceptibility varies for ciproflo xacin, linezolid, imipenem, doxycycline, and
clarithromycin [20–22]. For pediatric patients, priority is given to oral agents with high bioavailability and
effective cutaneous tissue concentrations, while balancing safety considerations: avoiding
fluor oquinolones due to skeletal toxicity risks and restricting carbapenems to maintain intestinal
microbiota equilibrium. In this case, empirical therapy with clarithromycin, linezolid, and amikacin was
initially administered. The regimen was subsequently optimized based on susceptibility results, replacing
clarithromycin with doxycycline due to macrolide resistance mediated by erm(39) gene-induced
methylation of the 23S rRNA macrolide-binding site [23]. A pediatric-specific risk control system was
implemented, including low-dose intermittent amikacin administration [24, 25] combined with
otoacoustic emission monitoring to mitigate cumulative dose-related ototoxicity and nephrotoxicity,
alongside doxycycline therapy limited to ≤ 21 days [26]. The dynamic therapeutic strategy featured an
intensive intravenous combination phase lasting 6 weeks, followed by an oral step-down consolidation
phase for 12 weeks [8, 27]. Additionally, this strategy is supported by six surgical debridements: weekly
during the acute phase and biweekly during the chronic phase. Therapeutic efficacy was determined
through dual quantitative criteria: three consecutive negative wound cultures and ≥ 80% granulation
tissue coverage, ultimately achieving complete wound healing through this four-month multimodal
intervention.
Conclusions
This case underscores the risk of NTM infections from dog bites and highlights mNGS's role in rapid
diagnosis. Effective treatment involves susceptibility-guided antimicrobial therapy and surgery, with
vigilant monitoring for adverse effects. Given the limitations of current guidelines and this single-case
report, further multicentric studies are needed to refine tr eatment protocols. Clinicians should be alert to
zoonotic NTM transmission and consider it in refractory cases.
