SUMMARY
A previously healthy woman in her late 40s presented 
with rapidly progressive sepsis 4 days after a dog bite. 
She developed disseminated intravascular coagulation, 
pancytopenia and acute kidney injury, ultimately 
progressing to complete anuria and dialysis dependence. 
Blood cultures confirmed Capnocytophaga canimorsus 
bacteraemia. Despite an initial clinical improvement with 
antibiotics and supportive care, she developed sudden 
isolated haemolysis and persistent thrombocytopaenia. 
Laboratory and renal biopsy findings confirmed the 
presence of a thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA), and 
complement- mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome 
(CM- HUS) was diagnosed. Following plasma exchange 
and initiation of eculizumab therapy, haematological 
remission was achieved, and her kidney function partially 
recovered.
This case highlights C. canimorsus infection as a rare 
trigger of CM- HUS in immunocompetent adults and 
outlines the patient’s diagnostic workup, the challenges 
of differential diagnosis and the complexity of both the 
clinical course and therapeutic approach in case of TMA 
following sepsis. 
BACKGROUND
Capnocytophaga   canimorsus   is   a   slow-   growing,   
encapsulated,   gram-   negative   rod   found   in   the   
normal   oropharyngeal   flora   in   dogs   and   cats.   
Human  infections  are  rare,  and  transmission  can  
occur via bites, scratches or even close contact. Clin-
ical presentations are highly variable, ranging from 
isolated  meningitis  to  fulminant  sepsis,  dissemi-
nated  intravascular  coagulation  (DIC),  multiorgan  
failure  and  thrombotic  microangiopathy  (TMA).  
Despite  broad  antibiotic  susceptibility,  mortality  
can approach 30%.
1 2
C.  canimorsus  is  the  Capnocytophaga  species  
most frequently linked in invasive zoonotic disease 
in  humans.
3
  Diagnosis  can  be  delayed  due  to  its  
fastidious  nature  and  slow  growth  in  cultures,  
prompting  increasing  use  of  molecular  methods,  
such as real- time nanopore sequencing.
4 5
In  our  case,  C.  canimorsus  infection  presented  
with  septic  shock,  severe  leukopaenia  and  coagu-
lopathy  consistent  with  DIC.  Although  the  initial  
clinical  course  was  dominated  by  sepsis-  related  
multiorgan  involvement,  the  later  development  of  
microangiopathic   haemolytic   anaemia   (MAHA),   
thrombocytopenia   and   worsening   acute   kidney   
injury (AKI) suggested a superimposed TMA, high-
lighting   how   complement-   mediated   haemolytic   
uraemic  syndrome  (CM-  HUS)  may  be  obscured  
during the early sepsis and DIC phase. 
CASE PRESENTATION
A  previously  healthy  woman  in  her  late  40s,  with  
two prior uncomplicated pregnancies, presented to 
the emergency department with a rapidly worsening 
clinical condition. Four days prior to admission, she 
had  been  bitten  by  her  dog  on  the  upper  lip  and  
received  tetanus  vaccination  as  prophylaxis.  She  
was prescribed phenoxymethylpenicillin (Apocillin) 
on  the  day  of  the  incident;  however,  antibiotic  
treatment  was  not  initiated,  as  she  was  unable  to  
collect her prescription. She developed a high fever 
(>40  °C),  malaise,  reduced  oral  intake  and  later  
sought medical attention when numbness and disc-
olouration  of  her  feet  appeared.  At  primary-  care  
emergency, she was found to have hypoglycaemia, 
refractory  to  oral  glucose  and  glucagon,  with  a  
nadir of 0.6 mmol/L, prompting urgent transfer to 
our emergency department.
On  admission,  she  was  alert  and  responsive  
but  appeared  septic  with  mottled  and  cool  lower  
extremities. She had not used any herbal remedies 
or other substances, and no relatives had been ill or 
presented with similar symptoms. The initial labo-
ratory  workup  revealed  marked  systemic  derange-
ment with severe inflammation, pancytopenia, AKI, 
liver enzyme elevation and coagulopathy. Key find-
ings are summarised in table 1.
Blood  gas  analysis  revealed  metabolic  acidosis  
with  a  pH  of  7.27  (7.35–7.43)  and  an  elevated  
lactate  level  of  5.7  (<2.5  mmol/L).  She  remained  
hypoglycaemic  despite  high  doses  of  intravenous  
glucose,  thought  to  be  sepsis  related.  On  arrival,  
the patient maintained adequate diuresis; urinalysis 
showed protein (2+) and blood (3+).
Given her constellation of symptoms—including 
fever,  pancytopenia,  coagulopathy  with  AKI  and  
lactic  acidosis—her  condition  was  interpreted  as  
DIC secondary to bacteraemia and early sepsis (quick 
Sequential   Organ   Failure   Assessment   (qSOFA)   
score 1/3). A surgical cause of her mottled extrem-
ities—including necrotising fasciitis—was ruled out 
after  clinical  and  radiological  evaluation.  Due  to  
the  severity  of  her  presentation,  broad-  spectrum  
intravenous  piperacillin/tazobactam  (Tazocin)  was  
started to cover dog- associated pathogens, including 
Capnocytophaga  species.  Notably,  C.  canimorsus 
was suspected from the onset and was 7 days later 
confirmed  by  both  aerobic  and  anaerobic  blood  
cultures.  She  was  subsequently  transferred  to  the  
intensive care unit (ICU) for closer monitoring.
Given   the   pancytopenia   and   coagulopathy,   
haematology  was  consulted.  The  differential  diag-
noses  included  acute  leukaemia  and  haemophago-
cytic   lymphohistiocytosis.   Haemolytic   uraemic   
syndrome  (HUS)  was  also  considered  but  seemed

unlikely  due  to  leukopaenia,  inflammatory  markers,  normal  
haptoglobin  and  non-  compensatory  reticulocytes.  Flow  cytom-
etry was performed, and bone marrow biopsy planned; however, 
normal results—with no increase in CD34+ blasts or abnormal 
phenotypes—led  to  cancellation  of  the  biopsy.  Clinically,  the  
presentation was consistent with sepsis. While awaiting results, 
supportive   thrombocyte   and   fibrinogen   transfusions   were   
administered.
After transfer to the ICU, she became completely anuric with 
rapidly worsening renal function. Aggressive fluid resuscitation 
and concurrent administration of loop diuretics were attempted 
without  success.  She  subsequently  developed  septic  cardiomy-
opathy  with  fluid  overload,  requiring  the  initiation  of  contin-
uous renal replacement therapy. Autoimmune tests (antinuclear 
antibodies  (ANA)  and  antineutrophil  cytoplasmic  antibodies  
(ANCA)) were negative.
On day 3 in the ICU, she developed diffuse skin bleeding with 
ecchymoses and distal cyanotic and ischaemic changes affecting 
several  toes  and  fingers,  though  pulses  and  sensation  were  still  
preserved.  These  findings  were  consistent  with  microthrom-
botic  phenomena  in  the  context  of  DIC.  Despite  multiorgan  
involvement, she never required vasopressor support beyond the 
stress- dose corticosteroids administered during the initial septic 
phase. She also received albumin infusions alongside other blood 
products.
After  8  days  of  hospitalisation,  she  was  transferred  to  the  
nephrology ward for ongoing dialysis- dependent AKI and intra-
venous antibiotic therapy. On transfer, the patient was clinically 
stable but anuric, with generalised oedema and multiple haema-
tomas,  yet  alert  and  oriented.  Antibiotics  were  de-  escalated  to  
intravenous ampicillin and later to oral Apocillin based on blood 
culture results.
On  the  nephrology  ward,  labs  showed  improving  inflam-
matory  profile,  reactive  leukocytosis,  recovering  platelets  and  
stable  haemoglobin.  Kidney  function  tests  showed  persistent  
azotaemia, while coagulation parameters normalised, indicating 
resolving  coagulopathy.  Table  2  summarises  her  laboratory  
results at that stage.
After   initial   improvement,   the   clinical   picture   changed   
when  she  developed  a  sudden  isolated  drop  in  haemoglobin  
(111 →  68  g/L  over  6  days)  without  evidence  of  bleeding  or  
haemodynamic  instability.  Platelets  remained  persistently  low  
(30–40×10⁹/L),  haptoglobin  became  undetectable  and  LDH  
levels rose, indicating ongoing haemolysis (figure 1).
A  direct  antiglobulin  test  (DAT)  (Coombs  test)  was  positive  
for  IgG,  raising  suspicion  of  autoimmune  haemolytic  anaemia.  
High-  dose  prednisolone  was  started  alongside  routine  prophy-
laxis,  and  several  blood  transfusions  were  given  over  2  days.  
When the haemolytic process failed to respond to corticosteroid 
therapy  and  platelets  remained  low  (41–46  ×  10⁹/L),  further  
evaluation  was  warranted.  A  peripheral  blood  smear  revealed  
15%–20%  schistocytes,  consistent  with  MAHA  suggestive  of  
TMA (figure 2). The initial DAT was likely a false positive from 
penicillin or prior transfusions; repeat testing was negative.
Complement  studies  and  ADAMTS13  activity  were  sent  for  
analysis.  Locally  available  standard  complement  tests  (C3/C4)  
were  within  normal  limits,  and  functional  assays  of  the  clas-
sical,  alternative  and  lectin  pathways  were  likewise  normal.  
ADAMTS13  activity  was  reported  shortly  after,  at  43.5%,  
effectively  ruling  out  thrombotic  thrombocytopenic  purpura  
(TTP), as TTP typically presents with activity below 10%. While 
awaiting  these  results,  plasma  exchange  (PEX)  was  initiated  as  
bridging therapy prior to the first dose of eculizumab.
Once  the  thrombocyte  count  had  increased  to  77×10⁹/L,  a  
kidney  biopsy  was  performed  showing  severe  acute  tubular  
necrosis, ischaemic glomeruli with fibrin thrombi and scattered 
crescent formation, consistent with TMA.
The  diagnostic  findings  in  this  case  were  best  demonstrated  
by  the  light  microscopy  features  shown  in  figure  3.  Immuno-
fluorescence and electron microscopy are not shown, as immu-
nofluorescence  revealed  no  immune  complex  deposition  and  
electron  microscopy  demonstrated  no  evidence  of  primary  
glomerulopathy.

OUTCOME AND FOLLOW-UP
Following  the  complex  medical  management,  the  patient  is  in  
good  general  condition  with  adequate  urine  output.  Kidney  
function remains reduced (serum creatine 175 μmol/L), and she 
will  be  followed  in  the  nephrology  outpatient  clinic  (figure  4). 
She  continues  maintenance  eculizumab  (1200  mg  intravenous  
every 2 weeks), for 4- months postinduction. Extended comple-
ment genetic testing did not identify any pathogenic variants; the 
results were communicated verbally by the reference laboratory, 
with  the  formal  written  report  pending  at  the  time  of  submis-
sion. Based on this information, complement blockade is being 
continued due to the severity of her disease, with planned reas-
sessment  for  discontinuation  if  renal  function  stabilises.  Iden-
tification  of  pathogenic  complement  gene  abnormalities  would  
favour longer term therapy with close monitoring for relapse.

However, no such variants were identified in this patient.
DISCUSSION
Differential diagnosis of TMA
TMAs encompass several overlapping clinical entities.
The initial priority in adults presenting with MAHA, throm-
bocytopenia and AKI is to exclude thrombotic TTP, a condition 
defined  by  severe  ADAMTS13  deficiency  (<10%).  Clinical  
scores like PLASMIC can help triage while awaiting the results, 
but  diagnostic  confirmation  is  laboratory  based.  Our  patient’s  
PLASMIC  score  was  4/7  (low  pretest  probability  of  TTP),  and  
ADAMTS13  activity  was  43.5%,  making  TTP  unlikely  and  
guiding further diagnostic workup.
8–11
STEC-  HUS  is  another  important  consideration.  The  patient  
tested  negative  for  Shiga  toxin  by  PCR.  Thus,  the  absence  of  
prodromal  enteric  illness  and  negative  microbiological  testing  
argues against STEC- HUS.
12
Ultimately,    the    constellation    of    persistent    haemolysis,    
schistocytosis,  dialysis-  dependent  AKI  and  kidney  biopsy  find-
ings  consistent  with  TMA  supported  a  diagnosis  of  CM-  HUS  
(figure 5). The diagnosis was further strengthened by the exclu-
sion  of  STEC  by  PCR  and  her  rapid  clinical  improvement  
after  initiating  eculizumab  therapy.  Her  insignificant  medical  
history  weakens  the  hypothesis  of  a  pre-  existing  complement  
defect requiring a ‘second hit’ and supports infection- triggered 
CM- HUS.
CM-  HUS  is  a  clinicopathological  diagnosis  of  exclusion  and  
complement  levels  (C3/C4)  may  be  normal  during  the  disease  
course.
6  7
  Importantly,  normal  complement  levels  (C3/C4)  do  
not  exclude  CM-  HUS,  as  levels  may  remain  within  reference  
ranges during the disease course. Notably, haemolysis appeared 
only after sepsis began improving, suggesting infection- triggered 
CM- HUS rather than pre- existing disease.
In   our   patient,   basic   complement   tests   (C3/C4)   and   
ADAMTS13 activity were obtained immediately before the first 
PEX session. The extended complement testing was performed 
8   days after the final PEX session and 4 days after the first dose of 
eculizumab. The observed reduction in the alternative pathway 
is  most  consistent  with  treatment  effects  rather  than  intrinsic  
complement depletion or activation.
Pathophysiology of CM-HUS: complement dysregulation and 
infectious triggers
CM- HUS (historically termed ‘atypical HUS’) arises from dysreg-
ulated activation of the alternative complement pathway, often 
in  genetically  predisposed  individuals.  Between  50%  and  70%  
of patients harbour genetic variants or autoantibodies affecting 
complement  regulatory  proteins,  such  as  factors  H,  I  and  B  or  
membrane cofactor protein (CD46).
13–15
 However, disease pene-
trance is incomplete, and clinical manifestation typically requires 
an external trigger, most commonly infections, pregnancy, auto-
immune disease or certain medications.
13–19
Although rarely reported, C. canimorsus can activate comple-
ment pathways, particularly in susceptible hosts. Notably, C. cani-
morsus displays variable serum susceptibility, with many isolates 
being   complement   sensitive   while   some   capsule-   expressing   
strains  show  partial  resistance,  which  means  that  complement  
activity  is  an  important  component  of  host  defence.
20  21
  This  
creates  a  clinical  paradox:  while  complement  activation  clears  
the  pathogen,  it  may  also  trigger  uncontrolled  endothelial  
damage and TMA in predisposed individuals.
This case adds to a small but growing body of literature linking 
C.  canimorsus  to  complement-  mediated  TMAs.
22–25
  A  partic-
ularly  illustrative  report  by  Bjørkto  et  al
22
  described  a  patient  
with C.  canimorsus-  induced  CM-  HUS  harbouring  a  comple-
ment  factor  B  variant,  successfully  treated  with  eculizumab.  
In  contrast  to  the  previously  reported  case,  no  pathogenic  or  
likely  pathogenic  complement  gene  variants  were  identified  in  
our patient, supporting infection- triggered CM- HUS without a 
demonstrable inherited complement predisposition.
Therapeutic strategy and clinical dilemma
Management  of  CM-  HUS  in  the  context  of  active  infection  
presents unique challenges. Eculizumab, a monoclonal antibody 
targeting  terminal  complement  component  5,  is  the  standard  
of  care  for  CM-  HUS  and  markedly  improves  haematological  
and  renal  outcomes,  particularly  when  initiated  early.  Delayed  
initiation  is  associated  with  incomplete  renal  recovery  and  
consequently  higher  risk  of  progression  to  end-  stage  kidney  
disease.
26–28
In  contrast,  PEX  is  lifesaving  in  TTP,  but  provides  only  
temporary benefit in CM- HUS, mainly by removing circulating 
complement  fragments,  inflammatory  mediators  or  autoanti-
bodies,  without  correcting  the  underlying  complement  system  
dysfunction.
29  30
  In  our  patient,  diuresis  returned  after  the  
final  PEX  sessions  but  before  eculizumab,  reflecting  only  tran-
sient stabilisation. Sustained haematological and renal recovery 
occurred only after complement blockade.
A  key  therapeutic  dilemma  is  using  complement  blockade  
during  active  bacteraemia,  as  terminal  complement  compo-
nents  are  critical  for  defence  against  encapsulated  bacteria—
including Neisseria   and   Capnocytophaga—raising   concerns   
about  eculizumab-  associated  infectious  risks.
31  32
  In  this  case,  
eculizumab  was  initiated  after  blood  cultures  became  negative,  
minimising this risk of uncontrolled infection. Vaccination, anti-
microbial  prophylaxis  and  close  monitoring  remain  essential  
when using complement inhibitors.
CONCLUSION AND BROADER IMPLICATIONS
Early recognition of CM- HUS and prompt initiation of comple-
ment  blockade  are  the  most  important  predictors  of  favour-
able  outcome.  Our  patient  achieved  haematological  remission  
and partial renal recovery, consistent with the current evidence

showing that renal function and time- to- initiation of eculizumab 
therapy are the main determinants of prognosis.
Although C. canimorsus-  associated  CM-  HUS  is  extremely  
rare,  this  case  highlights  the  importance  of  considering  infec-
tious  triggers—including  unusual  ones—in  patients  presenting  
with new- onset TMA. Improved awareness and molecular diag-
nostics may lead to the identification of more such cases.
Learning points
 ►Capnocytophaga canimorsus infection can trigger 
complement- mediated haemolytic uraemic syndrome (CM- 
HUS) in immunocompetent adults.
 ►Thrombotic microangiopathy may be masked during, or 
emerge late after, apparent sepsis recovery.
 ►Early initiation of eculizumab therapy improves 
haematological and renal outcomes in CM- HUS.
 ►Complement blockade should be initiated after achieving 
infection control.
