ABSTRACT
Humans have coexisted with dogs for at least 20,000 years, yet the biological conse-
quences of long-term human-dog co-residence remain poorly understood. We propose 
that sustained exposure to dogs may have contributed to context-dependent variation 
in human stress regulation, immune function, and socio-emotional neurobiology 
through environmentally responsive epigenetic mechanisms. Here, we define an epige-
netic imprint as detectable differences in gene-regulatory marks, including DNA methy-
lation at environmentally sensitive loci, consistent with developmental plasticity and 
early-life environmental calibration rather than germline inheritance. In this 
Commentary, we integrate evidence from genomics, neuroscience, microbiome 
research, evolutionary anthropology, and palaeoepigenetics to examine whether multi-
species living environments may represent an under-recognised biological exposure 
shaping human regulatory biology. We further outline a framework to test whether 
archaeologically inferred dog co-residence is associated with epigenetic and regulatory 
signatures in ancient human populations while accounting for major ecological and 
demographic confounds. Overall, we argue that human-dog cohabitation provides 
a plausible and testable model for investigating how long-term social and ecological 
relationships may influence stress and immune regulation across populations.

Commentary
Dogs represent the earliest domesticated species to have formed enduring symbiotic relationships with 
humans. Genetic evidence places their emergence alongside humans well before the advent of farming, with 
ancient genomes revealing a distinct canine lineage by at least 20,000 years ago [1,2]. Their spatial, 
behavioural and emotional intimacy endured across the Late Pleistocene and into the Holocene, embedding 
dogs deeply within human ways of life. Yet, in the expanding landscape of human evolutionary science, one 
dimension remains curiously absent: whether such sustained multispecies proximity generated measurable 
epigenetic signatures in human populations. Here, epigenetic signatures or imprints denote context- 
associated shifts in regulatory marks (especially DNA methylation) consistent with within-lifetime plasticity 
or early-life programming rather than transgenerational inheritance. Findings from modern biology suggest 
that this is biologically plausible and testable. Dogs are not passive associates; rather, they actively modulate 
human stress systems, social neurochemistry and microbial environments.
If epigenetic mechanisms encode long-term responses to social and ecological conditions [3–6], then the 
presence of dogs for tens of millennia constitutes a major, underappreciated axis of human genomic 
regulation. Human-cat symbiosis, however, follows a markedly different evolutionary and archaeological 
trajectory [7]. Whereas dogs accompanied humans for at least 20,000 years, domestic cats entered human 
societies only within the last two millennia. This temporal and functional asymmetry suggests that human- 
dog relationships represent a uniquely deep and developmentally embedded form of interspecies

In the present discussion, we distinguish among (a) within-lifetime epigenetic plasticity (environmen-
tally responsive regulation within an individual); (b) developmental programming (durable regulatory states 
established during prenatal/early-life windows); and (c) true transgenerational epigenetic inheritance via 
germline transmission across generations in the absence of direct exposure. This Commentary primarily 
focuses on (a) and (b) as plausible routes by which dog-rich ecologies could leave detectable, tissue-specific 
regulatory correlates in ancient and modern humans. We refer to these as context-linked regulatory 
correlates, i.e., population-level shifts in DNA methylation at environmentally responsive regulatory regions 
that track measurable contextual variables (e.g., dog co-residence intensity, household animal density, or 
co-burial indicators) rather than a claim of dog-specific causation from any single marker. Hence, we do not 
assume that dog-associated epigenetic marks were stably inherited through the human germline. Rather, 
similar exposures recurring over many generations could repeatedly re-induce comparable regulatory states 
without requiring germline transmission. Thus, our claims concern phenotypic plasticity and develop-
mental programming with potentially detectable ancient methylation correlates; in other words, here we do 
not claim genetic canalization or directional selection driven by dogs.
Archaeology indicates that dogs were present alongside humans, while genomics shows that they moved 
and dispersed with human populations. Over the past decade, genomic studies have also established the 
deep-time origins of dog domestication. What remains unclear, however, is whether the integration of dogs 
coincided with measurable shifts in stress and immune calibration in humans and/or dogs. 
Palaeoepigenetics offers a means to test this hypothesis by comparing regulatory profiles across well- 
defined archaeological exposure gradients. The emerging field of palaeoepigenetics uses characteristic 
damage patterns in ancient DNA to reconstruct premortem DNA methylation maps [8]. The most direct 
starting point is to examine context-linked methylation differences in present-day humans across well- 
characterised dog co-residence gradients (and, where possible, paired dog data), while controlling for 
lifestyle and socioeconomic confounds. Indeed, establishing a reproducible modern signal would provide 
the necessary baseline to motivate deep-time questions. Palaeoepigenetics would then serve as a second- 
stage test of whether similar regulatory correlates appear in archaeological settings where dog integration 
can be independently inferred. This would assess generalisability across distinct ecologies and lifeways, 
rather than assuming continuity. Practically, this can be addressed using epigenome-wide profiling of blood 
or saliva in modern cohorts with quantified dog exposure (duration, co-residence intensity, early-life 
exposure), along with endocrine/heart rate variability (HRV) measures and household microbiome sam-
pling. In modern populations, HRV can be measured alongside cortisol and oxytocin to quantify whether 
dog exposure is associated with stress buffering at the body level.
Studies show that methylation patterns in ancient human bones and hair are sufficiently conserved and 
interpretable to distinguish regulatory differences between past and present populations, and even to infer 
aspects of phenotype and environment [9,10]. While this approach comes with caveats (e.g., tissue 
specificity is not fully recoverable, and only changes established early in development are reliably seen), it 
opens a conceptual door. Indeed, methylation profiles reconstructed from ancient skeletal tissue are not 
direct readouts of acute physiological state, and current methods cannot infer overall stress levels with high 
specificity. Therefore, our proposal does not assume that dog exposure can be uniquely identified from bone 
methylation alone, but rather that it can be evaluated as a context-linked association within a broader multi- 
exposure framework. Here, we envision a staged research path. Such approach first requires establishing 
which classes of environmental variation are detectably associated with skeletal methylation in ancient 
datasets (e.g., broad differences in diet, mobility, pathogen burden, or settlement structure) under rigorous 
controls. Second, it requires evaluating whether multispecies co-residence indicators including dogs explain 
additional variance beyond these established factors, using within-site contrasts and negative controls. Of 
note, dogs are highlighted here not as a uniquely diagnostic exposure, but as a historically well-documented

co-resident species that generates clear, archaeologically inferable gradients for hypothesis testing. Thus, the 
strongest tests would combine independent evidence from archaeological proxies and ancient microbiomes 
with skeletal methylation, rather than relying on methylation alone.
Archaeologists no longer have to treat stress, sociality, and pathogen exposure as invisible, but they can, 
in principle, ask whether individuals embedded in dog-dense households differ epigenetically from those in 
dog-poor settings. Here, we use dog-dense and dog-poor as reproducible, dataset-specific categories of 
inferred dog co-residence intensity, defined from a standardized proxy index rather than raw counts 
alone. Candidate components of this index may include (where available): (a) relative abundance of dog 
remains within domestic deposits (e.g., proportion of canid remains among identified faunal remains); (b) 
recurrence of dog remains across occupation layers; and (c) spatial association with domestic features or 
household deposits. Contexts can then be classified using pre-specified thresholds within a site or dataset 
(e.g., lower vs. upper quantiles, or standardized scores), because absolute specimen counts are not directly 
comparable across sites with different excavation extent, preservation, and recovery methods.
Prehistoric dog lineages have been shown to track human migrations with remarkable fidelity, dispersing 
from eastern Eurasia and later admixing across western Eurasia, the Levant and the Arctic [2,11]. This 
finding reflects repeated generations of cohabitation, co-migration, and co-foraging. For humans, this 
means dogs were a stable component of the psychosocial and microbial environment in which children 
grew up, adults negotiated risk, and communities organized work and warfare. Such patterns indicate that 
dogs accompanied humans not as peripheral animals but as embedded members of foraging bands, sharing 
campsites, food resources and thermal shelter. Indeed, co-dispersal is not, by itself, diagnostic of compa-
nionship; many commensal and domesticates can track human movement. Here we treat co-dispersal as 
evidence of repeated opportunities for close co-residence, while the form of interaction (e.g., commensal, 
and affiliative) is expected to vary across ecologies and periods. Archaeological cases such as the Bonn- 
Oberkassel burial, where a dog was interred with two humans around 14,000 years ago, speak to relational 
dimensions that exceed mere utility [12,13]. Although human-canid co-burials should not be read as 
universal evidence for companionship, they provide a conservative way to identify a subset of settings 
where close relational investment is plausible. In this framework, co-burials function as stratification 
variables (high-integration vs. low-integration) rather than as stand-alone proof. Therefore, the persistence 
of such relationships over millennia positions dog proximity as one of the more stable features of human 
lifeways and developmental ecology across the late Pleistocene and Holocene. Moreover, dog proximity 
should not be equated with modern pet companionship. Across societies (and likely across prehistory) 
human-canid relationships plausibly ranged from commensal scavenging and loose tolerance, to task- 
focused cooperation (hunting/guarding), to intensive domestic co-residence and affiliative bonding. Hence, 
our claim is not that ancient dogs were uniformly ‘companions,’ but that variation in the intensity and type 
of co-residence created different psychosocial and microbial exposures, which can be operationalised and 
tested archaeologically.
Modern behavioural neuroscience offers a mechanistic explanation for why dogs may have had parti-
cularly significant regulatory effects on humans. For instance, mutual gaze between dog and owner triggers 
a positive-feedback oxytocin loop, increasing oxytocin concentrations in both species in a manner analo-
gous to parent-infant bonding [14]. More recent controlled comparisons indicate that this effect is strongly 
shaped by the dogs’ life experiences and relationship history rather than domestication alone. When wolves 
and dogs are raised under comparable social conditions, differences in oxytocin release are subtle, and 
wolves can also engage in close affiliative contact with bonded humans, even if the mutual-gaze-linked loop 
is less consistently expressed [15]. Together, this body of work indicates that the human-dog connection is 
not a fixed product of domestication, but a dynamic relationship reinforced through repeated social contact.
Notably, prolonged selection for affiliative temperaments and hypersociality in dogs likely amplified 
oxytocin-enhancing social circuits while concurrently reducing cortisol reactivity during interspecies 
interactions (domestication hypothesis; see [14,16–18]). Oxytocin is deeply intertwined with stress buffering, 
social trust and affiliative behaviour [19]. Oxytocin enhancement through routine dog interaction provides 
a biologically plausible pathway through which dogs, among many determinants, modulate human endo-
crine profiles. Furthermore, positive dog-owner interactions not only increase oxytocin, but often reduce 
cortisol in owners also [20], embedding dog proximity directly into the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal 
(HPA) axis stress response system. These are precisely the domains where environmentally responsive

epigenetic regulation (i.e., DNA methylation and chromatin changes at glucocorticoid, oxytocin, and 
immune genes) has been widely implicated [21,22]. In parallel, interaction with dogs was shown to reduce 
human cortisol levels, mitigate physiological markers of stress and promote parasympathetic activity [23]. 
Moreover, tactile interaction may represent another central mechanistic pathway underlying these effects, 
as affiliative touch between humans and dogs has been associated with increased oxytocin signalling, 
reduced cortisol levels, and changes in autonomic regulation [20].
If dogs are treated as active partners in a shared regulatory ecology, then the dog’s physiology should also 
reflect sustained co-residence. Human-dog interaction has been shown to co-modulate endocrine and 
autonomic states, with changes in oxytocin and cortisol observed on both sides of the dyad and with 
coupling that varies by familiarity, relationship history, and context [14,15,20,23]. Beyond hormones, the 
dog’s stress responsivity and affiliative behaviour are shaped by developmental experience and household 
environment. This suggests that ‘domestication’ effects are expressed through plasticity rather than fixed 
traits [15]. These bidirectional dynamics motivate a complementary palaeoepigenetic question: in settings 
where humans and dogs shared enclosed dwellings over long periods, would paired ancient samples show 
convergent regulatory profiles at environmentally responsive loci relevant to stress and immune function? 
Even if ancient dog methylomes are not always recoverable, modern paired-dyad work provides 
a mechanistic rationale for expecting interspecies alignment in endocrine state, microbial exposure, and 
potentially epigenetically sensitive pathways.
More specifically, these outcomes may involve epigenetically sensitive loci, including NR3C1 (glucocor-
ticoid receptor), FKBP5 (FK506 binding protein 5) and oxytocin receptor (OXTR) genes known to respond 
to social support, early caregiving and chronic stress exposure. Even without ancient methylomes, the 
mechanistic evidence motivates a modern first-stage test, which in turn can justify targeted palaeoepigenetic 
comparisons. Considered in the context of regulatory biology and downstream health consequences, the 
signal associated with dog proximity becomes even more intriguing. In a Swedish nationwide cohort, dog 
ownership predicted reduced all-cause and cardiovascular mortality, particularly among individuals living 
alone. This observation suggests that dogs function as long-term social buffers with measurable health 
consequences [24] (Figure 1). Nevertheless, human-dog interactions are not uniformly stress-reducing; 
costs, conflict, fear, resource competition, disease risk, and unpredictable animal behaviour can also elevate 
stress. Hence, we treat dog exposure as a bidirectional regulator whose net direction likely depends on 
setting, familiarity, workload, and social role.
While residual confounding effects are inevitable, the convergence of experimental and population-level 
evidence places dog companionship directly within the domain of biologically consequential social envir-
onments that may be linked to more favourable cardiovascular profiles, possibly via increased physical 
activity and psychosocial buffering. Neuroscientific investigations extend these findings to the brain. 
Interactions with familiar dogs engage reward, attachment, and prefrontal-limbic regulatory circuits 
which mirror activation patterns seen in close human relationships [25]. Conversely, dogs’ perceptual 
experience of humans can be inferred from the strong neural responses in the animal’s caudate nucleus, 
a brain region associated with positive expectations, to human faces [26]. These convergent neural 
responses suggest that long-term human-dog proximity may have recurrently calibrated pathways for 
social bonding, vigilance, and emotional regulation through developmental and within-individual plasticity. 
Indeed, any contribution to genetic evolution via selection remains speculative and would require separate 
evidence. If such circuits were repeatedly engaged within individuals living in dog-rich social ecologies, dogs 
could have acted as persistent neurobiological partners that reinforce affiliative motivation and stress 
buffering. Across deep time, similar developmental environments recurring in many communities could 
repeatedly re-induce comparable endocrine and immune regulatory states (potentially leaving detectable 
correlates in ancient skeletal methylation) without requiring germline inheritance.
If dog proximity shaped human stress neurobiology, it also likely shaped human immunology. High- 
impact microbiome studies reveal that cohabiting humans and dogs share significant fractions of their 
bacterial taxa [27]. Accordingly, dogs alter the microbial composition of household environments, increas-
ing microbial richness and introducing environmental species that are otherwise uncommon in industria-
lised settings. Early-life exposure to dogs, in particular, has strong effects on the infant gut microbiome and 
is associated with reduced risk of allergies and atopic disease in later life [28,29]. These findings are central 
to the argument: microbial metabolites (especially short-chain fatty acids such as butyrate, and acetate)

Extending this perspective, such mechanisms raise a compelling possibility that ancient human com-
munities living in tight co-residence with dogs may have experienced distinct immune-microbial ecologies, 
leading to divergent epigenetic trajectories across inflammation-related genes. In settings such as 
Palaeolithic shelters, Neolithic houses or Arctic winter dwellings, dogs would have acted as vectors of 
environmental microbial diversity which shaped human immune training from infancy. The notion that 
dog domestication influenced human immune epigenetics aligns closely with emerging ‘One Health’ 
frameworks [34], which conceptualise human and animal biology as intertwined rather than separate. 
Within this perspective, multispecies cohabitation is not a peripheral detail of human evolution but a core 
ecological condition that shaped microbial sharing, pathogen pressures, and immune calibration across 
generations. The One Health framework emphasizes these reciprocal exchanges by studying the ways in 
which humans and companion animals form shared microbial ecosystems that can influence physiology, 
disease susceptibility, and even the regulatory architecture of immune genes [34].
The hypothesis that dog domestication produced distinct immune-microbial human environments gains 
particular relevance with the recent progress of palaeoepigenetics. A landmark study by Gokhman and 
colleagues reconstructed Neanderthal and Denisovan DNA methylation maps from ancient remains which 
shows that cytosine deamination patterns preserve signals of original epigenetic states [35]. Interestingly, 
subsequent work has used these maps to reconstruct Denisovan anatomical traits, indicating the functional 
interpretability of ancient regulatory variation [36]. Because ancient methylomes are typically reconstructed 
from non-brain tissues, brain relevance in the present commentary refers to downstream neuroendocrine 
and socio-emotional pathways inferred from regulatory targets, not direct measurement of brain methyla-
tion. While current methods are constrained by tissue type and preservation quality, they are sufficiently 
robust to compare methylation landscapes across ancient human populations. With these methods avail-
able, a new question comes into focus: Can ancient methylomes detect the regulatory consequences of 
multispecies companionship? If dog proximity consistently modulated stress, bonding and immune systems 
in modern humans, similar effects may have occurred in prehistoric populations, effects that could remain 
encoded in skeletal methylation patterns. Sites rich in human-dog cohabitation evidence, such as co-burials, 
dog cemeteries integrated with human graves, settlements with substantial dog management, provide clear 
targets for future testing. We, therefore, frame the prediction as context-linked somatic/developmental 
regulatory signatures (methylation differences as regulatory marks), not as the stable inheritance of dog- 
induced epigenetic marks through the human germline.
Several explicit hypotheses can be drawn from current evidence. First, individuals from dog-intensive 
contexts may show methylation signatures consistent with stress buffering, such as altered regulation of 
NR3C1 or FKBP5, both heavily studied in research on early-life adversity and social support [37–39]. 
Second, long-term engagement in interspecies caregiving may have influenced methylation of oxytocin 
receptor genes, which are environmentally responsive and crucial for social bonding. Third, early-life 
exposure to dog-mediated microbial diversity may yield immune-related epigenetic profiles distinct from 
populations with limited dog contact, particularly in Toll-like receptor pathways and cytokine gene 
promoters. Fourth, because ethnographic research indicates sex- and role-based differences in dog hand-
ling, including men hunting with dogs and women and children engaging in domestic caretaking, epigenetic 
differences may be structured by age and gender. These correlates are defined a priori as differences that 
scale with dog co-residence and persist under within-site contrasts (i.e., comparisons between dog-rich vs. 
dog-poor settings within the same settlement) and negative controls. Finally, where paired humans and 
dogs remains are available from the same setting, interspecies concordance may be detectable, such that 
dog-rich settings show more similar stress- and immune-linked methylation patterns across humans and 
dogs than dog-sparse settings. It is noteworthy that we do not expect a dog-specific epigenetic signature; we 
test whether dog co-residence explains additional variance beyond general stress proxies and other 
covariates.
To increase falsifiability and reduce confounding, we frame the hypotheses as context-linked predictions 
with explicit negative controls and competing explanations. These include:

●

Dyadic concordance test: In paired human-dog remains from the same household/burial setting,

under negative controls (e.g., dogs present at the site but not co-resident).
Finally, because effect sizes may be modest, adequately powered tests will likely require pooling across sites 
and, critically, complementary modern cohort studies as an initial validation step.
Notably, the above hypotheses are ambitious but scientifically grounded. Their pursuit would integrate 
ancient genomics, neuroendocrinology, and microbiome science in unprecedented ways. More importantly, 
they would challenge entrenched assumptions that domestication processes shaped humans primarily 
through cultural and behavioural channels. If the human-dog relationship influenced stress resilience, 
immune maturation or social neurobiology at the regulatory level, then domestication becomes not only an 
economic or symbolic partnership but a molecularly entangled
developmental ecology, in which multispecies co-residence can calibrate stress, immune, and social 
regulatory systems. This commentary reframes dogs not merely as companions or tools but as long-term 
components of the human developmental environment by representing co-regulators of physiology, 
sociality and health. Recognising this influence would expand the present understanding of human 
biological plasticity and highlight the need for multispecies frameworks in biological anthropology and 
human ecology. As palaeoepigenetic datasets grow, the key question is whether dog-rich versus dog-poor 
settings show detectable and reproducible differences in regulatory profiles, and how robust those associa-
tions remain after accounting for diet, mobility, pathogen load, and other co-varying exposures.
Final reflections and future directions
The emerging molecular record invites a new generation of questions about the depth and 
biological significance of human-dog relations. Companionship-based influences on gene regulation 
invite several lines of inquiry. One concerns the specific epigenetic signatures that might distin-
guish prehistoric communities with intensive dog integration from those with minimal or no 
canine co-residence. Another involves identifying the developmental periods, such as prenatal, 
infant, or adolescent ages, during which dog proximity exerted the strongest regulatory effects, 
and whether these influences varied by sex or social role. Long-term interactions with domesticated 
dogs may also have gradually shaped human neurobiological systems implicated in social bonding, 
threat detection, and stress regulation, potentially contributing to shifts in oxytocinergic and 
prefrontal-limbic architecture over millennia. In this perspective, dog cohabitation is unlikely to 
produce simple, mark-for-mark epigenetic inheritance across generations. However, by persistently 
altering stress biology, immune exposure, and caregiving environments, long-term dog – human 
co-residence could contribute to transgenerationally stable regulatory phenotypes through repeated 
developmental programming within a shared ecological niche. Furthermore, integrating palaeoepi-
genetic profiles with ancient microbiomes could clarify the shared ecological niches occupied by

humans and dogs. More broadly, domesticated animals may have played a role not only in cultural 
evolution but also in the molecular calibration of human stress physiology, immunity, and social 
neurobiology. The next decade of paleoepigenetic research might therefore determine whether the 
companionship that shaped human culture also shaped human gene regulation. If ancient methy-
lomes reveal even subtle differences between populations with and without close canine partners, 
dogs must be understood not only as ‘man’s best friend,’ but as long-term environmental regulators 
who contributed to human stress resilience, immune development, and social neurobiology.
