Abstract
A woman in her 60s presented with a three-week history of progressive subacute back pain without neurological deficits. MRI of the
lumbar spine demonstrated necrosis at the L3–L4 disc and adjacent vertebral bodies, raising suspicion for malignancy or infection. Two
image-guided bone biopsies were nondiagnostic. A peripheral infectious disease workup was similarly unremarkable. Owing to the
persistent concern for infection, 16S ribosomal RNA sequencing was performed on biopsy samples, which identifiedCapnocytophaga
canimorsus. Only after receiving this result did the patient recall a minor dog bite sustained six weeks prior to symptom onset. She was
managed with an extended course of oral antibiotics with complete clinical recovery. This case illustrates the capacity ofC. canimorsus
to cause insidious vertebral osteomyelitis in immunocompetent individuals, underscores the diagnostic utility of 16S rRNA sequencing
in culture-negative infections, and highlights the critical importance of a detailed exposure history in atypical clinical presentations.
Keywords:vertebral osteomyelitis;Capnocytophaga canimorsus; immunocompetent
Introduction
C. canimorsusis a slow-growing Gram-negative anaerobic bac-
terium commonly found in the oral mucosa of cats and dogs.
It is a zoonotic pathogen transmitted to humans through
bites or scratches, leading to infections at various body sites,
including vertebral osteomyelitis [
1–3]. Although such infections
are most frequently observed in immunocompromised patients,
they may also occur in immunocompetent hosts [
4]. Due to
its fastidious growth in microbiological media, polymerase
chain reaction (PCR) is considered the gold standard diagnostic
tool for detectingC. canimorsusinfections [5].Capnocytophaga
species are typically sensitive to penicillins, third-generation
cephalosporins, carbapenems, clindamycin, chloramphenicol,
and doxycycline. Post-exposure prophylaxis is recommended in
immunocompromised patients [
5].
Case report
A woman in her early 60s was referred by her general practitioner
with a three-week history of progressive back pain, without focal
neurological symptoms. She denied fever, infective symptoms, or
trauma, and reported no weight loss, anorexia, or B symptoms.
She later recalled a minor dog bite sustained six weeks prior, while
intervening in a dog attack, which healed quickly and was initially
disregarded.Her past history was unremarkable except for remote
melanoma excision, and there was no other history of immuno-
suppressive conditions. Clinical examination revealed focal lower
lumbar spine tenderness without neurological deficits.
On admission, her white cell count was 12.1×10
∧
9/L with
neutrophils 9.17×10
∧
9/L, but subsequently normalized. Her
CRP was 51 mg/L (<5 mg/L), ESR 45 mm/hr (<20 mm/hr),
and procalcitonin 0.26
μg/L (0.5–2.0). Other haematological,
biochemical,  oncological,  and  rheumatological  evaluations
were normal. An initial community MRI (
Fig. 1A) demonstrated
abnormal marrow signal intensity involving the L4 vertebral
body and pedicle, the L3/L4 intervertebral disc, and the inferior
endplate of L3, without aggressive bone destruction. Repeat MRI
performed two weeks later (
Fig. 1B) showed enhancement and
bone marrow signal loss within L3 and L4 vertebral bodies show-
ing definite progression consistent with discitis-osteomyelitis
without abscess formation. A PET scan, undertaken due to
melanoma history, revealed progressive bone erosion and a new
right paraspinal intensely avid soft tissue lesion suggestive of a
phlegmon (Fig. 2).
A CT guided percutaneous needle biopsy, performed two
days after the repeat MRI, demonstrated only cartilage tissue,
consistent with sampling error. A repeat CT guided biopsy two
weeks later showed suppurative inflammatory exudate; however,
microscopy and cultures, including for acid-fast bacilli, were
negative. Given the high suspicion of infection, stored second
biopsy specimen was subjected to 16S ribosomal RNA testing
at the Royal Brisbane and Women’s hospital laboratory, which
identifiedC. canimorsus.

fort. Follow-up MRI (

with endplate irregularity and marrow oedema. Three months
later, MRI at her local hospital (
Fig. 1D) demonstrated persistent
but improving changes consistent with osteomyelitis/discitis with
partial healing. Given her clinical recovery, ongoing observation
was recommended without further imaging.
Discussion
Capnocytophagaspecies  are  recognized  to  cause  local  and
systemic  infections  predominantly  in  immunocompromised
patients, though cases in immunocompetent individuals have
also been reported. There are previous reports of vertebral
osteomyelitis caused byCapnocytophagain immunocompetent
patients with oral infection [
6], as well as sepsis with vertebral
osteomyelitis in immunosuppressed patients [
4,7]. Our patient
was immunocompetent, maintained good oral hygiene, remained
afebrile without infective symptoms, and experienced symptom
onset more than six weeks after a minor dog bite, underscoring
the indolent nature of the pathogen even in healthy hosts.
Capnocytophagais a slow-growing organism that is difficult to
cultivate and may evade conventional blood cultures [
8]. Given
this fastidious nature, PCR—particularly 16S rRNA sequencing—
is a superior diagnostic tool [
9]. In this case, both tissue cultures
were negative,and only 16S rRNA testing confirmed the pathogen.
Management ofCapnocytophagainfections depends on the
site, severity, and patient comorbidities. Effective antimicrobial
options includeβ-lactams/β-lactamase inhibitor combinations,
tetracyclines, clindamycin, fluoroquinolones, penicillins, line-
zolid, and rifampicin [
5,10]. Although treatment duration is not
clearly defined, antimicrobial therapy of at least 6–8 weeks is
generally recommended for bone and joint infections [
5]. Our
patient was initially treated with ciprofloxacin but switched to
Augmentin Duo Forte due to adverse effects, ultimately achieving
recovery after a 12-week course.
This case highlights thatCapnocytophagacan cause indolent
yet clinically significant infections in immunocompetent patients,
underscoring the importance of obtaining a thorough exposure
history in relevant clinical contexts. Moreover, when infection is
strongly suspected and conventional testing is negative, 16S rRNA
testing should be considered to establish the diagnosis and guide
targeted antimicrobial therapy.
