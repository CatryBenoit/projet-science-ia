The  presence  of  dogs  in  Portugal  is  confirmed  by  zooarchaeological  evidence  dating  to  at  least  7600  years  ago.  The  11  native  
dog breeds that exist in this territory are therefore the end- product of a complex evolutionary process. This study characterises 
the  genomic  diversity  and  population  structure  of  10  native  breeds  based  on  whole-  genome  sequencing  (WGS)  data  obtained  
from 46 unrelated dogs (4–6 per breed, 9–16x coverage). These data were merged with public genomes and also downsampled to 
match the 170 K Illumina Canine HDBeadChip, including an additional 1770 dogs from 211 dog breeds for a worldwide compre-
hensive population genomics analysis. The results revealed multiple maternal haplotypes distributed across haplogroups A- D, 
high  autosomal  diversity,  low  to  moderate  inbreeding,  breed-  specific  genomic  signatures  of  evolutionary  events,  and  shallow  
genetic differentiation ranging from greater homogeneity to intra- breed sub- structure. These results reflect the diverse history of 
Portuguese dog breeds, such as geographical isolation in the Castro Laboreiro Watchdog, recent admixture between the Algarve 
Barrocal and Portuguese Warren Hound, shared ancestry in the Alentejo Mastiff, Transmontano Mastiff and Estrela Mountain 
Dog  breeds,  and  a  severe  demographic  bottleneck  in  the  Portuguese  Water  Dog.  This  research  emphasises  the  importance  of  
preserving these breeds for their contribution to the overall genomic diversity of dogs, functional utility and cultural relevance, 
providing the basis for outlining strategies for the conservation and genetic management of locally well- adapted dog breeds.
1   |   Introduction
The oldest archaeological evidence of dogs (Canis lupus familia-
ris) in Portugal dates to ~7600 years ago through the excavation 
of two almost complete skeletons (Arias et al. 2 017, 2015; Detry 
and Cardoso 2010; Pires et al. 2019), corresponding to the Late 
Mesolithic  chronology  in  Western  Europe.  Since  then,  many  
more  dog  remains  have  been  found  in  archaeological  contexts  
throughout  the  Iberian  Peninsula  (e.g.,  Albizuri  et  al. 2019), 
suggesting  human's  interest  in  maintaining  and  using  these  
animals  as  symbols  in  rituals  (e.g.,  Daza-  Perea 2019;  Valera  
et al. 2010), or as assistants. A study on Iberian dogs dated to the 
Chalcolithic  period  (ca.  5000–4000  BP)  detected  some  degree  
of  morphological  variability  based  on  height-  at-  shoulders  esti-
mations,  with  medium-  to-  large  dogs  likely,  reflecting  selection  
pressures for size differentiation and probably selected for tasks 
such as hunting or livestock guarding (Blaschikoff et al. 2022). 
Evidence  of  accentuated  dog  morphotypes  in  Portugal  can  be  
dated  to  the  10th  century  (Rodrigues 1993)  and  the  12th  cen-
tury (Braga 2000). In other regions of Europe, dogs dated from at

ing,  and  hunting.  The  first  dog  show  in  Portugal  took  place  
in  Oporto  in  1902  (Alpoim 1999),  while  the  first  breed  stan-
dards  date  to  the  beginning  of  the  20th  century  in  the  “Livro  
de Origens Português” (L.O.P.) book, founded in 1936 and first 
published in 1955 (Alpoim 1999; de Vasconcelos 1995).
Portuguese  dogs  are  classified  according  to  their  morphology/
function/behavior in five distinct groups, according to the FCI 
classification (see Supporting Information S1: Section S1 for ad-
ditional information).
The  Portuguese  breeds  are  officially  recognized,  either  nation-
ally and/or internationally (exceptions are the Algarve Barrocal 
Dog and the Terceira Cattle Dog), and are in general character-
ized by small population sizes which are not subject to intensive 
artificial  selection.  Additionally,  they  are  not  closed  breeding  
populations since new genetic input can still be recruited based 
on breed- specific morphological and behavioural traits (related 
to their role) exhibited by individual dogs, mainly in rural areas, 
from  where  the  breeds  originated  (a  detailed  description  on  
the  rules  for  a  Registration  Book's  new  entry  can  be  found  in  
Supporting Information S1: Section S2).
Genetic  studies  on  Portuguese  dog  breeds  based  on  unipa-
rental  molecular  markers,  revealed  the  presence  of  several  
mitogenomes  distributed  through  clades  A  to  D  (Blaschikoff  
et al. 2022) confirming previous data on 8 breeds using a smaller 
mtDNA  fragment  (Pires  et  al. 2006),  as  well  as  Y  chromosome  
haplotypes  defined  through  Y-  Short  Tandem  Repeats  (STRs)  
combined   with   Y-   Single   Nucleotide   Polymorphisms   (Pires   
et al. 2 017). The analysis of autosomal genetic markers, such as 
STRs and Amplified Fragment Length Polymorphisms (AFLPs), 
revealed low levels of breed differentiation for a smaller dataset 
of Portuguese breeds (Pires et al. 2009). Expected heterozygosity 
levels showed that the least genetically diverse breeds were the 
Portuguese  Pointer  and  the  Portuguese  Sheepdog,  followed  by  
the  Castro  Laboreiro  Watchdog,  while  Transmontano  Mastiff  
was  the  most  diverse.  A  study  by  Van  Asch  et  al.  (2011)  using  
autosomal  STRs  confirmed  this  shallow  genetic  structure  for  
seven  Portuguese  dog  breeds  and  relatively  heterogeneous  ge-
netic compositions, preventing individual dogs ́ genotypes from 
being easily assigned to their breed of origin.
For  the  majority  of  the  Portuguese  dog  breeds,  animals  can  
be  subjected  to  distinct  selection  strategies:  in  one  group  the  
show/urban  dogs  are  bred  with  more  intensive  artificial  selec-
tion  for  breed-  specific  morphological/aesthetic  traits,  and  in  
another  group  selected  for  performance  according  to  specific  
function, for example, guarding, herding or hunting. According 
to  Pedersen  et  al.  (2013)  in  the  first  case  it  can  lead  to  higher  
inbreeding  levels  and  in  the  latter  case  to  the  maintenance  of  
greater  genetic  variation.  The  Portuguese  Water  Dog  is  an  ex-
ception,  because  due  to  new  sanitary  regulations,  they  are  no  
longer allowed into the fishing boats and therefore are not used 
anymore for its original function, that is, aid local fishermen in 
their traditional work/tasks.
High-  throughput  sequencing  (HTS)  technologies  have  enabled  
unprecedented  opportunities  to  study  dog  genomes.  Genomic  
information  has  become  fundamental  to  describe  patterns  of  
genetic diversity and differentiation, and infer historical demo-
graphic processes, such as expansions/contractions, admixture, 
and the degree of gene flow. A recent genomic analysis including 
Portuguese Livestock Guarding Dogs (LGDs) identified two con-
temporary lineages, multiple ancestries, and extensive gene flow 
among several modern LGD breeds (Coutinho- Lima et al. 2024). 
However,  an  in-  depth  genomic  analysis  to  understand  the  an-
cestral and phylogenetic relationships among all Portuguese dog 
breeds has not been carried out to date.
We  aimed  to  conduct  a  large-  scale  analysis  of  the  genomic  di-
versity and population structure of 10 Portuguese breeds, based 
on  high-  throughput  sequencing.  These  data  were  also  downs-
ampled  to  match  the  170  K  Illumina  Canine  HDBeadChip  for  
a  comprehensive  population  genomics  analysis  of  worldwide  
breeds. Such study allowed for a better understanding of the ori-
gins, breed relationships, and the evolutionary processes under-
lying breed differentiation.
2   |   Materials and Methods
2.1   |   Biological Samples
For   the   Algarve   Barrocal,   Portuguese   Pointing   Dog,   and   
Transmontano  Mastiff  breeds  (n  =    12),  we  collected  five  mil-
liliters  of  peripheral  blood  from  the  cephalic  vein  by  vacuum  
puncture into EDTA- containing tubes and stored at 4°C. Blood 
collection  was  carried  out  by  licensed  veterinarians  with  the  
consent of dog owners. For the remaining breeds, we used DNA 
samples  (n  =    40)  available  from  a  biobank  established  by  our  
team in 2004 at Instituto de Investigação Agrária e Veterinária 
(INIAV, IP), Portugal.
Genomic  DNA  was  extracted  from  0.2  mL  of  blood  using  the  
Jetflex genomic DNA purification kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) 
following manufacturer recommendations. DNA libraries were 
prepared  from  a  minimum  of  1.0  μg  of  genomic  DNA  using  
NEBNext  DNA  Library  Prep  Kit,  following  the  manufacturer's  
recommendations.  Brief ly,  genomic  DNA  was  randomly  frag-
mented  to  approximately  350  bp  by  shearing,  end-  polished,  A-  
tailed,  ligated  to  NEBNext  adapters  for  Illumina  sequencing,  
indexed,  and  PCR-  enriched  using  P5  and  indexed  P7  oligos.  
Paired- end (2 ×   150 bp) shotgun resequencing on NovaSeq 6000 
instruments   (Illumina)   was   outsourced   to   Novogene   (UK).   
Details  on  the  sampling  and  genomic  data  of  10  Portuguese  
(n =    46) and 4 foreign dog breeds (n =    11) generated in this study 
are provided in Table 1; Figure S1; and Tables S1 and S2.

For the Portuguese Water Dog, genomic data from a US popula-

Further  details  on  the  dataset  and  methodological  approaches  
are in Figure S2.
Unless   stated   otherwise,   the   results   were   plotted   with   R   
Statistical  Software  v4.3.1  (R  Core  Team 2023)  using  the  pack-
age ggplot2 v3.5.1 (Wickham 2016).
Data normality and variance homogeneity were assessed using 
Shapiro  and  Levene's  tests,  respectively.  For  analysis  involving  
comparisons between two groups, Student's t- test were used for 
normally  distributed  data,  whereas  non-  parametric  Wilcoxon  
tests,  including  the  Wilcoxon  signed-  rank  test  for  paired  data  
and the Wilcoxon rank- sum test for independent data, were ap-
plied  when  normality  assumptions  were  not  met.  For  compar-
isons  involving  more  than  two  groups,  a  one-  way  analysis  of  
variance (ANOVA) was used under normality assumptions; oth-
erwise, the non- parametric Kruskal- Wallis test was used. When 
ANOVA results were statistically significant, a post hoc pairwise 
Tukey HSD test was conducted to identify specific group differ-
ences.  In  cases  where  the  Kruskal-  Wallis  test  was  significant,  
a post  hoc  pairwise  Wilcoxon  rank-  sum  test  was  conducted.  A  
significance level of α = 0.05 was applied for all statistical tests. 
These statistics were performed using R.
Pearson correlation coefficients and statistical significance val-
ues were estimated using the cor.test function in R. If the data 
were  not  normally  distributed,  Spearman  correlation  coeffi-
cients were used instead.

2.3   |   Whole- Genome Sequencing and Variant 
Calling
The quality of the reads was checked using the FastQC v0.11.9 
software  (Andrews 2010).  Cutadapt  v1.18  (Martin 2011)  was  
used  to  remove  adapter  sequences,  discard  reads  <  35  bp  and  
trim  low-  quality  bases  (Q  <    30)  from  read  ends.  Filtered  reads  
were  mapped  to  the  dog  mitochondrial  and  nuclear  reference  
genome  assemblies  CanFam3.1  (GenBank  accession  numbers  
NC_002008.4 and GCA_000002285.2, respectively) using BWA 
MEM  v0.7.17  (Li  and  Durbin 2009)  with  default  settings.  The  
alignments  were  indexed  and  sorted  with  SAMtools  v1.7  (Li  
et al. 2009). Reads with mapping quality (MapQ) of 30 or greater 
were   kept.   AddOrReplaceReadGroups   and   MarkDuplicates   
tools  from  PICARD  v2.18.14  (Broad  Institute 2019)  were  used  
to  add  read  groups  and  to  remove  duplicate  reads,  respec-
tively.  A  set  of  known  nuclear  variants  from  dbSNP  build  151  
mapped to the Canfam3.1 reference genome (5 648 530 SNPs and 
Indels) was used to perform GATK v4.1.7 (McKenna et al. 2010) 
BaseRecalibrator with default arguments.
For the nuclear variants, individual GVCF files were generated 
using  GATK  HaplotypeCaller.  GATK  GenomicsDBImport  and  
GenotypeGVCFs were used to consolidate the variants of all in-
dividuals  and  perform  joint  variant  calling  to  produce  a  single  
multisample  VCF,  respectively.  Variants  with  low  quality  (QD  
<    6 and GQ <    20), as well as low depth of coverage (DP < 5), were 
removed  using  GATK  VariantFiltration.  Only  biallelic  SNPs  
were selected using the GATK SelectVariants tool.
Missing  data  for  each  individual  and  SNPs,  sample  depth  and  
minor  allele  frequency  per  SNP  were  accessed  using  a  custom  
bash  script.  These  results  (Figure S3)  were  visualized  using  a  
custom  R  Markdown  script.  For  population  genetic  analysis,  a  
threshold to exclude low- quality samples and SNPs was selected 
(see Figure S2 for more details).
SNPs  annotation  and  additional  statistics  on  the  distribution  
of SNPs per breed are described in Supporting Information S1: 
Section S4.
For a comprehensive analysis of Portuguese and worldwide dog 
breeds,  the  57  newly  generated  genomes  (Table S2)  and  pub-
lished whole- genome sequence data for 96 dogs from 25 breeds 
and  2  Iberian  wolves  (Table S4)  were  downsampled  to  overlap  
the Illumina CanineHD 170 K SNP array—the data mapped to a 
genome assembly other than CanFam3.1 were converted using 
liftOver (Hinrichs 2006), prior to downsampling. The resulting 
dataset  was  merged  with  another  1674  dogs  representing  195  
worldwide breeds and 9 wild canids previously genotyped on the 
Illumina CanineHD SNP array (see Table S3) and allowed for a 
better representation of each breed. The datasets were combined 
with PLINK v1.9 (Purcell et al. 2007) (default version used, un-
less stated otherwise), addressing strand inconsistencies and tri-
allelic positions by using - - flip and - - exclude, respectively, prior 
to  merging.  The  raw  merged  dataset  containing  1838  individ-
uals  (Table S5)  and  163  886  autosomal  SNPs  was  then  filtered  
following the FAO guidelines (Ajmone- Marsan et al. 2023) and 
using PLINK. SNPs with a call rate <    95% and individuals with a 
call rate < 90% were excluded, as well as SNPs with MAF < 1%. 
Pairwise  kinship  coefficients  were  estimated  using  the  KING  
method  (Manichaikul  et  al. 2010)  implemented  in  PLINK  v2.0  
(Chang  et  al. 2015).  Pairs  with  kinship  coefficients  >  0.177,  in-
dicative  of  first-  degree  relationships  (parent-  offspring/full  sib-
lings)  were  identified  and  one  individual  from  each  pair  was  
removed.  A  total  of  1767  individuals  and  140  426  autosomal  
SNPs  were  retained  for  downstream  analyses.  Unless  stated  
otherwise,  all  analyses  with  SNP  array  data  used  a  minimum  
of  3  samples  and  a  maximum  of  10  randomly  selected  samples  
per  study—to  avoid  size/source-  related  bias  affecting  phyloge-

pairs with median sharing above the 95th percentile, circle plots

2.12   |   TreeMix Analysis
The  TreeMix  v.1.1  (Pickrell  and  Pritchard 2012)  software  was  
used  to  investigate  evidence  of  gene  flow  between  Portuguese  
dog  breeds.  To  accurately  estimate  allele  frequencies,  all  sites  
with missing data were removed. A cluster- stratified frequency 
for  each  marker  was  calculated  using  PLINK  with  the  options  
- - freq  and  - - family.
To account for LD and considering that SNP density in the SNP 
array  data  is  on  average  1SNP/20Kb,  SNPs  were  clustered  into  
groups of 300 using the flag “- k”, exceeding typical LD lengths 
in  modern  dogs  (Yang  et  al. 2019).  To  assess  confidence  in  the  
inferred tree, blocks of 300 SNPs were resampled with replace-
ment using the flag - - bootstrap.
Maximum  likelihood  (ML)  trees  allowing  one  to  N  migrations  
(m), where N is the total number of populations, were generated 
10 times per edge count using random seeds (−seed). Standard 
errors of migration rates were calculated with the “- se” option, 
and the “- global” option was used to optimize the migration net-
work. The Golden Jackal was used as an outgroup.
The  OptM  R  package  v0.1.8  (Fitak 2021)  was  used  to  estimate  
the  optimal  number  of  migration  edges.  Then,  1000  bootstrap  
replicates  of  the  best  m  were  run  to  ensure  statistical  power  
using  a  script  from  the  R  (v4.4.1)  package  BITEV2  v2.1.2  
(Milanesi  et  al. 2 017).  Functions  treemix.bootstrap, plot_resid 
and treemix.drift from the R package BITEV2 were used to plot 
the tree graph, residuals' error and drift for the optimal migra-
tion, respectively.

In  this  study,  46  genome-  wide  sequence  data  (29  males,  17  fe-

A  total  set  of  12  782  087  high-  quality  autosomal  SNPs—mostly

In  the  Principal  Component  Analysis  (PCA)  based  on  WGS  
(Figure 5a, Figure S18a), PC1 and PC2 explained 6.25% and 6.02%

statistically  significant  (p-  value  0.61  and  0.10031,  respectively;  
Table S17), as expected in genome- wide data with many loci con-
tributing minimally. The explained variance is therefore limited. 
A separation of the most differentiated breeds is observed. PC1 al-
lowed for the separation of the Castro Laboreiro Watchdog breed. 
PC2 allowed for the separation of the Portuguese Sheepdog, Castro 
Laboreiro  Watchdog,  and  two  hunting  dog  breeds—Portuguese

ST

their moderate inbreeding (≥ 10%; Figure S18b). Additionally, PC2 
captured the sub- structure within the Portuguese Warren Hound 
dogs related to body size (height at withers), separating the large 
and medium- sized dogs from the small ones. A central cluster of 
breeds was resolved after the removal of the most isolated breeds, 
allowing  for  the  separation  of  the  Portuguese  Water  Dog,  Saint  
Miguel  Cattle  Dog,  and  small-  sized  Portuguese  Warren  Hound

investigate  if  the  observed  patterns  can  be  explained  by  demo-
graphic processes.

Watchdog  shows  a  distinct  genomic  profile  (see  PCA  analysis

At K  =    7  the  hunting  breed  Portuguese  Pointing  Dog  formed  
its own cluster, followed by the Saint Miguel Cattle Dog (K = 8) 
(from  the  Azores  Archipelago)  and  the  Algarve  Barrocal  Dog  
(K =    9). The Portuguese Pointing Dog and Algarve Barrocal Dog 
maintained  highly  homogeneous  admixture  proportions  up  to  
K =  15.
Evidence  for  intra-  breed  population  structure  was  detected  in  
the  Portuguese  Water  Dog  (K  =    2),  splitting  the  breed  into  two  
distinct  clusters—the  Portuguese  and  the  US  populations—
which  are  geographically  and  genetically  isolated  populations.  
This  differentiation  is  probably  due  to  a  founder  effect  and  the  
high inbreeding that followed the establishment of the US pop-
ulation  (see  section  Limited  inbreeding  and  moderate  levels  of  
autosomal genomic diversity). This result was already evident by 
the PCA analysis (Figure 5b) and is corroborated by the Treemix 
(Figure S23).
At  higher  Ks,  from  K  =    10  onwards,  an  intra-  breed  stratifica-
tion  was  observed  and  may  reflect  the  existence  of  two  breed-
ing groups. It was possible to identify the cause for such signal: 
for  the  case  of  the  Estrela  Mountain  Dog  (K  =    11),  it  could  be  
an  effect  of  a  breeding  lineage  associated  with  the  hair  type  
(Table S21),  and  in  the  case  of  the  Castro  Laboreiro  Watchdog  
(K  =    12)  to  the  origin  of  the  specimens,  that  is,  pedigree  dogs  
(show dogs) versus working dogs (mainly rural dogs) (Table S2).
Noteworthy   was   the   heterogeneous   pattern   shared   by   the

an  admixed  ancestry  and  a  close  evolutionary  affinity  (SNP

The   IBS   genetic   distance   estimated   from   140   426   SNPs   in

clades  (Figure 6a,  Figure S25,  Table S22).  Each  multi-  breed  
clade  comprised  two  to  18  breeds  that  clustered  together  with  
>    50% bootstrap support (82% on average; exception of the small- 
sized  Portuguese  Warren  Hound  dogs,  Fonni's  dog,  Mannara's  
dog, Miniature Pinscher and Catahoula Leopard Dog, that were 
assigned to a clade with a support < 50%). The combined analy-
sis of the data generated in this study, including published data-
sets on other worldwide dogs (Tables S3 and S4) allowed for the 
refinement  of  the  dog  cladistic  relationships.  Compared  to  the  
phylogeny  of  Parker  et  al.  (2 017),  six  new  clades  emerged  and  
three  regrouped  with  others.  Nine  out  of  11  Portuguese  breeds  
were assigned to six distinct clades—Water Dogs, Pointers and 
Setters, Iberian Livestock Guarding Dogs, Sighthounds and re-

prised six to 15 breeds that clustered with > 60% support, (90% 
support  on  average,  exception  of  the  small-  sized  Portuguese  
Warren Hound dogs that were assigned to the Terrier clade with 
a support of 48%), reflecting shared function (e.g., Pointers) and/
or  morphology  (e.g.,  Terrier),  and/or  geography  (e.g.,  Iberian  
Livestock  Guarding  Dogs).  The  Saint  Miguel  Cattle  Dog  and  
Terceira Cattle Dog (represented by a single individual) could not 
be  assigned  to  any  clade  but  clustered  together  with  American  
dog  breeds  (e.g.,  Xoloitzcuintle).  Eight  out  of  11  breeds,  (ex-
cept  for  the  Terceira  Cattle  Dog,  Transmontano  Mastiff  and  
Alentejo Mastiff) formed well- supported monophyletic clusters 
(> 90%), indicating that they are genetically differentiated. The 
mentioned  individuals  that  were  not  grouped  by  breed,  often  
clustered  with  breeds  from  the  same  geographical  region  and/
or  function,  for  example,  Transmontano  Mastiff  close  to  the  
Spanish Mastiff.

The  Portuguese  LGDs  grouped  together  with  other  breeds  of  
the same type of function/behaviour and geography, such as the 
Pyrenean mastiff, Spanish Mastiff and Great Pyrenees, forming 
the Iberian Livestock Guarding Dogs' clade, reflecting regional 
adaptation (e.g., climate, orography, pathogens) and a close evo-
lutionary  history.  These  dogs  traditionally  accompanied  hu-
mans  and  livestock  (mainly  sheep  for  wool  production)  during  
historical  seasonal  transhumance  migrations,  by  foot,  across  
Iberia, primarily to protect the flocks from wolf attacks (Carreira 
dos  Santos 2025;  Carvalho  and  Marques 2020;  Coppinger  and  
Coppinger 2002; Mier and Tente 2018), often mating with other 
dogs from different regions/owners. Coutinho- Lima et al. (2024) 
detected  a  close  genetic  relationship  among  36  LGDs  breeds  
across Eurasia suggesting a shared ancestry and a high level of 
gene-  f low  among  them.  The  mentioned  transhumance  migra-
tions ended in 1999 in Portugal (Oliveira Cruz 2007). Presently, 
the  Transmontano  Mastiff  and  the  Castro  Laboreiro  Watchdog  
are  the  only  breeds  that  still  co-  exist  with  the  Iberian  wolf,  in  
the  sense  that  they  share  a  geographical  range.  For  these  two  
breeds,  dogs  are  selected  mainly  on  the  basis  of  functional  pa-
rameters, since their activity for flock protection against preda-
tors is still important.

geographical area (Alentejo). In such circumstances, mating op-
portunities were possible. Nevertheless, no reports of intentional 
matings between the two breeds were found in the bibliography 
nor from the breeders.
A  significant  intra-  clade  IBD  sharing  was  detected  between  
Transmontano Mastiff and the Spanish Mastiff Dog (Figure 6b). 
Coutinho- Lima  et  al.  (2024)  also  detected  a  closer  genetic  rela-
tionship  between  these  breeds.  Due  to  their  close  geographic  
range,  these  breeds  may  interact  more  often  with  each  other,  
and  individuals  from  both  breeds  can  be  seen  as  guardians  of  
the same f lock.
The  Portuguese  Sheepdog  is  the  only  sheepdog  breed  found  
in  the  Iberian  continental  flat  lands.  It  clustered  in  a  specific  
clade—the  Continental  herders  (node  support  of  95%).  Their  
two  other  Iberian  counterparts,  the  Pyrenean  Sheepdog  and  
the Catalan Sheepdog, originated in the Pyrenees mountainous 
area, clustered together (Figure 6a). The IBD analysis showed an 
inter- clade haplotype sharing with the Alentejo Mastiff (already 
seen  in  Treemix  (Figure S23))  and  Portuguese  Water  Dog  (re-
stricted to the Portuguese population, despite the TreeMix anal-
ysis (Figure S23) showing no support). According to Cabral and 
Romeiras (1930) the Portuguese Sheepdog could have descended 
from the Pyrenean Sheepdog and improved through the recruit-
ment  of  the  Briard,  a  French  breed.  Later,  Vasconcelos  (1995) 
suggested  that  the  Portuguese  Sheepdog  could  be  considered  
a  small-  sized  Portuguese  Water  Dog,  adapted  to  the  herding  
task, possibly through breeding with herding breeds such as the 
Pyrenean Sheepdog or Catalan Sheepdog. A high level of shar-
ing with the Pyrenean Sheepdog, Catalan Sheepdog and Briard 
breeds  could  be  observed,  although  not  significant  (<  51.7  Mb;  
Figure S27a).
The other two herding breeds, the Saint Miguel Cattle Dog and 
the Terceira Cattle Dog, are insular breeds, from the Azores ar-
chipelago. Although clustering together in the phylogenetic tree 
(node  support  >  90%),  they  could  not  be  assigned  to  any  clade,  
likely  due  to  the  fact  that  they  result  from  admixture  between  
several   Continental   breeds,   but   have   subsequently   evolved   
in  relative  isolation  on  the  islands  (Amaral  and  Veiga 2004; 
Molinari 2024). The phylogeny of the Terceira Cattle Dog relies 
on one individual—interpretation of these results requires cau-
tion. Amaral and Veiga (2004) mentioned that the Saint Miguel 
Cattle Dog resulted from the crossbreeding of the Iberian mastiff 
type dogs (the precursors of the present- day Livestock Guarding 
Dogs) as well as the Iberian Alano (extinct), resulting in a lighter 
type of molosser dog. Whereas Terceira Cattle Dog probably de-
scends from medium- sized, long- haired herding or hunting dogs 
brought  by  European  settlers,  which  were  used  to  control  and  
herd wild cattle (Oliveira 1998). By the mid- 19th century, the ar-
rival  of  other  foreign  breeds  (e.g.,  Dogue  de  Bordeaux,  Mastiff  
and  Bulldog),  influenced  the  Saint  Miguel  Cattle  Dog  (Amaral  
and  Veiga 2004).  Our  study  recovered  a  strong  signal  for  the  
Dogue  de  Bordeaux,  English  Mastiff  and  Bulldog  breeds,  al-
though not significant (< 51.7 Mb; Figure S27b). Instead, signifi-
cant IBD sharing was detected between the Saint Miguel Cattle 
Dog  and  the  Great  Dane  and  Boxer  breeds,  representatives  of  
the Mastiff type dog. Based on microsatellite data, (Rocha 2002) 
reported  genetic  affinity  between  the  Terceira  Cattle  Dog  and  
the  Saint  Miguel  Cattle  Dog,  which  is  supported  by  the  haplo-
type sharing results (Figure 6b). Although these results also re-
covered  a  significant  sharing  between  Terceira  Cattle  Dog  and  
Portuguese Water Dog (both US and Portuguese population), no 
historical reports were found to justify this genetic relationship.

sized  Portuguese  Warren  Hound  dogs  and  the  medium/large-  
sized  dogs  was  not  detected  (Figure 6b).  The  small  body  sized  
subtype  dogs  clustered,  at  the  basal  position,  of  the  Terriers  
clade,  apart  from  their  counterparts,  which  grouped  together  
in  the  Sighthound  and  related  breeds  clade  (Figure 6a).  This  
corroborates  the  within-  breed  genetic  sub-  structure  related  to  
body  size  in  this  breed,  suggesting  that  the  small-  sized  variety  
has  a  distinct  ancestry  probably  related  to  a  small  body  type.

which could have influenced the development of other European 
pointing breeds such as the English Pointer (with origin in the

together  with  Algarve  Barrocal  Dog  and  medium/large-  sized

The  Barrocal  Algarve  Dog,  clustered  within  the  “Sighthounds  
and  related  breeds”  clade,  was  monophyletic  to  the  medium/

in  showing  a  high  genetic  affinity  between  these  two  breeds  
(see  Admixture  (Figure 5c),  Treemix  (Figure S23),  Haplotype

results  from  occasional  crosses—undocumented  by  breeders  
since dogs from both breeds were used (and still are) in the same 
hunting packs. The putative influence from the Saluki breed, a 
potential  source  population  for  the  Barrocal  Algarve  Dog,  was  
not detected in this analysis.
In the present genomic study, no significant haplotype sharing 
between the Iberian wolf and the Portuguese native breed- dogs 
was  detected  (Figure 6b).  A  previous  study  has  reported  lim-
ited hybridization between dogs and wild wolves in the Iberian 
Peninsula, such occurrences being mostly restricted to the edge 
of  the  wolf  population  distribution  range  and  most  likely  with  
free- ranging dogs (Lobo et al. 2023).
Genomics and multi- analysis approaches play a valuable role 
in  unravelling  the  evolutionary  trajectories  of  domesticated  
animals.  In  summary,  this  study  captured  the  genetic  sig-
natures  in  the  genomes  of  the  Portuguese  native  dog  breeds  
and  supports  historical  records  of  their  origins  and  develop-
ment, revealing distinct evolutionary processes. For instance, 
shared  ancestry  and  extensive  gene  flow  have  predominated  
in the case of the Portuguese native LGDs, while local adapta-
tion and geographical isolation have contributed to their dif-
ferentiation.  Founder  effects,  genetic  drift,  and  more  recent  
exogenous influences have marked the evolutionary history of 
island  breeds.  The  Portuguese  Warren  Hound  exhibits  intra-  
breed  stratification  due  to  different  founder  populations,  ex-
ogenous  influences,  and  sexual  isolation  of  the  most  distinct  
small-  sized  dog  variety.  Evidence  of  admixture  (geneflow)  
could be recovered between the following breeds: the Algarve 
Barrocal Dog and the medium/large- sized Portuguese Warren 
Hound   breeds;   the   Alentejo   Mastiff   and   the   Portuguese   
Sheepdog  Dog,  and  between  the  Estrela  Mountain  Dog  and  
the Transmontano Mastiff. Demographic bottlenecks, founder 
effects,   isolation   by   distance,   genetic   drift,   and   inbreed-
ing  are  paradigmatic  of  the  Portuguese  Water  Dog  breed.  
Demographic  bottlenecks,  past  events  of  inbreeding,  and  a  
complex relationship with other breeds of its own phylogenetic 
clade  were  observed  in  the  case  of  the  Portuguese  Pointing  
Dog breed. The inbreeding levels in Portuguese breeds were, 
in general, of low concern; nevertheless, higher than 10%, and 
their  management  as  open-  breeding  populations  should  be  
maintained. Special actions should be implemented to retain 
the  genomic  diversity  observed.  To  prevent  further  genetic  
differentiation  between  the  US  and  Portuguese  populations  
within  the  Portuguese  Water  Dog  breed,  gene  flow  between  
these groups should be promoted.
The  evolutionary  processes  and  the  traditional  dog  selection  
practices,   mainly   based   on   functionality,   lead   to   ecotypes   
adapted  to  their  environments.  In  such  circumstances,  genetic  
variability was preserved more than homogeneity. The specific 
genomic  compositions  of  the  Portuguese  dog  breeds  can  be  re-
garded  as  an  important  reservoir  of  genetic  diversity  or  even  a  
source  of  genetic  variation  for  the  species  and  their  conserva-
tion should be assured. Portuguese native dog breeds are a good 
example  that  it  is  possible  to  maintain  distinct  and  functional  
breeds without relying on excessive inbreeding.
Breed-  specific  genetic  variation  in  association  with  adaptive  
and selective traits is certainly relevant to be addressed in future 
research.
